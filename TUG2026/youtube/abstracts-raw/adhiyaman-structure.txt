{Prasad Adhiyaman, Shreyans Babel, Chhandak Bagchi, Boris Veytsman}
{Inferring logical structure of a scientific paper from visual representation: An easy task for humans, but surprisingly hard for~\programrlap{\acro{AI}}}
{For several millennia people have used visual clues to convey the
logical structure of long texts.  The use of red color for section heads
can be traced to Egyptian papyri, and had been prominent in medieval
books.  The requirements of monochrome printing presses led to the use
of font sizes, series and special fonts to mark chapters, sections and
subsections.  Human beings tend to easily infer the logical structure
from them.

Accessibility equipment, like screen readers, requires the logical
structure to be explicitly specified in the document, e.g., in tagged
\PDF{}s. While \LaTeX\ logical commands like \cs{section}, \cs{subsection},
etc., define the logical structure, some authors use visual clues like
\cs{Large} or \cs{scshape} to mark up sections. This makes conversion of legacy
documents with visual formatting to logical formatting an important
task.

We trained \acro{BERT} and open source \acro{LLM}s for this task. To
train and test the models, we used a script to convert logically
formatted papers from arXiv to visual formatting, different for each
paper, but consistent throughout it. Then we asked the models to infer
the logical structure of the paper and perform the inverse
transformation.

To our surprise, the initial results were quite inaccurate. While
training improves the performance of models, it is still significantly
lower than that of humans.

Our hypothesis is that a problem might lie in the limited input window
of the models. A human reader first compose a ``bird's eye view'' of the
paper to learn the relation of its different parts. This view is lost in
the linear reading. If this hypothesis is right, it provides important
insights for accessibility and machine learning. First, the intuition of
the authors of accessibility standards that prioritized logical
structure for speech recognition devices may be right. Second, a
comprehension of a paper by a computer model may require several runs
through text with different levels of detail.
}
