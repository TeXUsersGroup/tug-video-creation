{Timothy Erickson, Aarush Bhat}
{Visual impairment meets mathematics: Bypassing institutional inertia through code-based workflows}
{While individuals with disabilities make up roughly 15\% of the general
population, they represent fewer than 3\% of employed \STEM\ professionals.
This staggering disparity is rarely due to a lack of capability, but rather
a lack of accessible infrastructure. When visually impaired students
encounter highly visual fields like mathematics and computing science, they
are frequently met with a reactive ``retrofit'' model of accommodation: a
scramble to translate complex non-linear materials at the last minute,
placing an exhausting, hidden tax on the disabled student's time and
cognitive energy.

In this talk, we explore the two-year collaborative journey of a veteran
Mathematics instructor and a blind Computing Science student at the
University of Alberta as they bypassed institutional inertia to engineer a
proactive, ``born accessible'' framework.

We approach this systemic challenge from two intersecting perspectives.
First, from the pedagogical side, we discuss the failures of traditional
university remediation. We detail the necessary shift toward ``mind's eye''
instructional vocalization, the use of tactile geometry tools, and why
standardizing on write-once code formats is essential for equitable
education.

Second, from the student and engineering perspective, we examine the
technical reality of surviving high-level \STEM. We highlight how
code-based authoring\Dash specifically Markdown, \LaTeX, \ASCII\ Math,
and \acro{JSON\slash YAML}\Dash serves as the ultimate equalizer. By
treating visual layouts as structured data, blind students can
completely bypass inaccessible \GUI\ software to programmatically
generate complex mathematics and database diagrams.

Finally, we look toward the frontier of accessibility. We will share
insights from our ongoing testing of accessible \PDF{}s via Lua\TeX,
alongside the current limitations of browser-based rendering. Furthermore,
we tease how emerging automation and Large Language Models (\acro{LLM}s)
can be safely leveraged to parse inaccessible visual ``eye candy'' into
structured, semantic markup, establishing a vision for the future of
automated spatial accessibility. Ultimately, we aim to demonstrate that
with the right combination of pedagogical adaptation and \TeX-based
workflows, post-secondary \STEM\ does not have to be a barrier for blind
students.}
